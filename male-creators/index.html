A man on camera is not the whole idea.

B0669 returned 2.18× reported purchase value per dollar spent. B0647, with a male presenter, returned 0.72×. Both use explanation-led storytelling. Being male alone did not make the second ad outperform.

Your takeaway: earn attention with her situation and a clear explanation. Gender is a perspective, not a substitute for the story.

More spend does not mean a stronger return.

B0152 delivered 687 purchases at 1.16× ROAS. B0647 delivered 2,130 purchases at 0.72×, with much more spend. Sales volume and efficiency tell different stories. B0152 also had a lower hook rate but a higher return: initial attention is not the same as buying intent.

Your takeaway: judge a video by purchases and cost as well as attention. A memorable opening still needs a useful payoff.

Gut ads: a longer story versus a short offer

0.54× ROAS · $232,199 spend · 1,603 purchases

A woman talking in her car describes clothes fitting in the morning and feeling tight later, then moves through failed attempts and an explanation. The customer situation is relevant, but this execution had a lower reported return.

B0437 · Short sale creative

1.02× ROAS · $53,312 spend · 654 purchases

The actual video is a 15-second sale montage. Its accompanying ad text carries the pants story. The short execution had a higher return in this window; this does not isolate length as the cause.

Use the pants moment because customers describe it. Don’t assume a long story will sell simply because the pain point is right. Reach the useful explanation and payoff without repeating the problem.

03

How you tell it as a man.

Speak like someone who understands the problem and can explain it clearly. The feeling to aim for is: “He’s talking about something I deal with.”

What she hears

What she sees

Normal clothes, a natural setting, eye contact and the product in your hands when you explain it.

What makes it yours

Your phrasing, pacing and personality. If your partner has experienced it, her story can give you a real starting point.

04

Build one story she can follow.

She recognizes the problem.

Use the story to connect her everyday experience with a clear product explanation. The opening gives her a reason to listen.

She already knows the product.

Try a concise product-detail or current-offer video. Get to what she wants to know without making her sit through the whole problem again.

A practical 45–60 second starting structure. The timing is a filming guide, not a proven formula. Tap each beat for words and shots you can adapt.

Keep the strength.

Name the problem early. Use a concrete scene. Explain one point clearly. Show what you’re talking about. Give the opening a payoff.

Cut what slows it down.

Vague “wellness” language, repeated symptoms, a list of ingredients with no explanation, or a sales pitch before she knows why it matters.

Make the next version more informative

Keep the same story and change one thing: the first sentence, the first visual, or how soon you reach the explanation. Compare purchase results after the videos have had meaningful delivery. If viewers stay but don’t buy, look at the product explanation, offer and landing page as well as the hook.

Our ad comparisons show what happened to whole ads. Changing one element at a time helps us learn which part is helping.

Your first video: One problem. One recognizable moment. One clear explanation. One simple next step. Tell real experiences as real experiences, and let your own delivery carry it.

Pick the story that feels natural and like you.
